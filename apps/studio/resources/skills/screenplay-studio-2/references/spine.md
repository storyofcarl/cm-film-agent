# Spine

The Spine is the one idea every other foundation document derives from. It is authored first and gated first. Its job is to guarantee that premise, theme, character wound, opposition, and world are not five competent-but-separate elements but **five views of a single thing**.

## Why this exists

Forgettable scripts are often well-built — sound premise, clean arcs, balanced structure — and still feel assembled rather than inevitable. The cause is almost always a missing spine: the elements were each validated against their own criteria and never forced to lock into one idea.

World-class scripts have a spine you can state in a sentence, and every major element is that sentence wearing a different costume:

- *Chinatown* — "Some corruption is too deep to fix; trying only spreads the damage." → Gittes's wound (couldn't save someone before), the water/incest conspiracy, the futile-detective structure, the sun-bleached corrupt LA.
- *The Godfather* — "You cannot touch evil without becoming it." → Michael's arc, the family business, the structure that ends where it begins inverted, the world of ritual and violence.
- *Whiplash* — "Greatness may require self-destruction, and that trade might be worth it (or monstrous)." → Andreas's want, Fletcher as opposition, the escalating-abuse structure, the airless conservatory world.

## The Spine Statement

One sentence. Not a logline (that's the premise). Not a theme statement alone. It is the **irreducible idea** the whole film is an argument about — stated so that you can see how character, conflict, and world all express it.

Format:
> **[A truth, tension, or question about how the world/people work], dramatized through [the core situation].**

The first half is the idea. The second half is how it becomes a movie. Both halves are required — an idea without dramatization is an essay; a situation without an idea is a plot.

## Choice-based authoring

Generate **2-3 divergent Spine candidates** from the user's seed concept — genuinely different *ideas the same material could be about*, not rewordings. Each candidate states:

1. **The Spine sentence.**
2. **The five derivations** (one line each): how premise, theme, protagonist wound, opposition, and world each express this spine.
3. **The muse**, if one fits: what existing film this spine evokes the *feeling* of.
4. **The tradeoff**: what this spine commits you to and what it forecloses.

Recommend one, with reasoning. The human selects or redirects.

Example seed: "a nurse who steals from patients."

> **Candidate A — "Care and predation wear the same uniform."** Premise: a hospice nurse beloved by families is quietly robbing the dying. Theme: the argument that tenderness can be the most effective disguise. Wound: she was unseen her whole life; theft is the only way she takes. Opposition: a grieving daughter who *likes* her. World: the intimate, trusting world of end-of-life care. Muse: the cold intimacy of *The Talented Mr. Ripley*. Tradeoff: commits to a slow, character-interior thriller — not a procedural.
>
> **Candidate B — "Everyone is stealing; she just got caught."** Premise: her theft exposes a hospital system that monetizes the dying. Theme: individual sin as the visible tip of institutional rot. Wound: she learned the rules from a system that taught her no one's watching. Opposition: the institution. World: for-profit healthcare machinery. Muse: the systemic chill of *Michael Clayton*. Tradeoff: bigger canvas, less intimate; opposition is harder to give a face.
>
> **Recommendation: A.** It locks tightest — the spine is *in* every element, and the daughter-who-likes-her gives the theme a credible opposing voice. B is strong but the institution diffuses the spine across too many targets.

## Validation

A Spine passes when:

- It is one sentence, with both halves (idea + dramatization).
- All five derivations are real, not forced — each element genuinely *is* the spine, not merely compatible with it.
- It is debatable — a thoughtful person could argue the opposite. (Spines that everyone already agrees with produce films with nothing at stake.)
- It is specific to this material — it could not be the spine of a dozen other films.

A Spine fails when it's actually a theme statement with no dramatization, a premise with no idea underneath, or so general it fits anything ("love conquers all," "crime doesn't pay").

## How downstream stages use it

Every later stage opens by reading the Spine and must demonstrate derivation:

- **Premise** is the spine made into a situation.
- **Theme** is the spine stated as an arguable claim, with opposition able to argue back.
- **Opposition** embodies the counter-argument to the spine.
- **Characters** carry the spine as personal wound and want/need tension.
- **World** is the environment that pressures the spine hardest.
- **Structure** is the shape that makes the spine's argument land.

If a downstream choice can't trace to the Spine, either the choice is wrong or the Spine is incomplete and must be revisited (human-gated).
