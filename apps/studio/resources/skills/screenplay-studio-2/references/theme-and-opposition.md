# Theme + Opposition

Theme and opposition are developed together because they are the same thing seen from two angles: **the theme is an argument, and the opposition is what argues the other side credibly enough to win.** A theme with weak opposition is a lecture; a theme with strong opposition is a film.

## Part 1: Theme as Argument

### The requirement

Theme is not a topic ("loyalty," "ambition," "grief"). It is a **claim** the film makes about that topic, stated so a thoughtful person could disagree.

- ❌ Topic: "This film is about ambition."
- ✅ Claim: "Ambition that requires cruelty is still worth it." (debatable — many would argue the opposite)

The claim must derive from the Spine. If the Spine is "greatness may require self-destruction, and the trade might be worth it," the theme-claim is the arguable proposition inside it.

### Dramatized, never stated

The single loudest AI tell in a screenplay is a character stating the theme aloud, usually in Act 3. World-class scripts dramatize theme through **choices under pressure** — the theme is *proven* by what characters do when it costs them, not *announced*.

Requirements:
- The theme must be expressed through at least one **irreversible choice under maximum pressure** (usually the climax).
- No character may state the theme directly. (Flag any line that does — see the AI-tells scrub.)
- The audience should be able to *argue about* what the film was saying — that's the sign it dramatized rather than declared.

### Validation
Theme passes when: it's a claim not a topic; it's debatable; it derives from the Spine; it lands through a choice, not a speech; and the opposition (below) can genuinely argue against it.

## Part 2: Opposition as Co-Equal Foundation

Opposition gets a foundation document of the **same depth as the protagonist** — not a state-change row in the outline. This is non-negotiable for world-class output: the protagonist is only as compelling as the force resisting them.

### The credible counter-argument

The opposition's core job is to **win the theme's argument** — to make the audience genuinely feel the other side. If the theme is "ambition requiring cruelty is worth it," the opposition embodies "no, it destroys everything that makes the achievement matter" — and embodies it *persuasively*.

- *The Dark Knight* — the Joker isn't a foil; he's a working argument that order is a fragile lie. The film is scary because he keeps almost being right.
- *There Will Be Blood* — Eli is a credible spiritual counter to Daniel's materialism; their war is the theme.

If the opposition can't win a single round of the argument, the theme is unearned.

### Opposition is not always a villain

The opposing force can be a person, an institution, a worldview, the protagonist's own appetite, the world's indifference, or time. What matters is that it (a) credibly opposes the want, and (b) credibly argues against the theme.

### Choice-based authoring

Generate **2-3 divergent opposition logics**, each anchored to a muse where useful, each with its tradeoff:

> **Option A — Institutional (leans *Michael Clayton*):** the opposition is a system, faceless and procedural. Tradeoff: hard to give a climax a face.
> **Option B — Personal mirror (leans *Heat*):** a single equal-and-inverse antagonist. Tradeoff: risks the obvious doppelgänger unless made specific.
> **Option C — Internal (leans *Nightcrawler*):** the protagonist's own appetite is the opposition. Tradeoff: needs strong external pressure to avoid going static.
> **Recommendation:** [one], because [derivation from Spine and theme].

### The Opposition document

Once selected, develop with protagonist-level depth:

- **Want** — what the opposition is actively pursuing (concrete, visual).
- **Logic** — why, from inside their view, they are *right*. Written so it's persuasive.
- **The counter-argument** — the exact claim against the theme they embody.
- **Wound/origin** — what makes their logic earned (even institutions have an origin logic).
- **Pressure applied** — specifically how they escalate against the protagonist across acts.
- **The round they win** — the moment the audience feels the opposition might be right. Required.
- **Specificity** — the concrete, non-generic texture (how they operate, their tells, what only this antagonist would do).

### Validation
Opposition passes when: it's developed to protagonist depth; its logic is genuinely persuasive; it embodies a real counter-argument to the theme; it has a moment where it wins a round; it escalates across the structure; and it's specific, not a stock villain.

## Per-stage specificity check
- **Obvious version:** what's the default antagonist for this premise? (the corrupt boss, the rival, the cartel) Name it.
- **Our version + source:** invented-specific or muse-evoked? Either passes.
- **Fail:** the stock villain with no specific logic and no winning round.
